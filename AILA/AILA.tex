\section{Probabilistic programs through the lens of linear logic}
Recent years have seen a surge of interest for probabilistic programming. Studying this kind of program poses difficult challenges already if we only consider discrete probabilistic choices (i.e Bernoulli random variables): as opposed to deterministic programs, each execution of a probabilistic program can give a different result both in term of convergence and of output; as opposed to non-deterministic programs, in probabilistic programs it is essential to keep track of quantitative properties: for example, a probabilstic program can have even infinitely many execution path which are not terminating and still have probability of termination 1.\\
The need of tracking quantitative information (and, in particular the number of time a probabilistic choice has been duplicated) hints that linear logic, with its resource-wise approach, offers a useful framework in this field. This has been confirmed by the work on the weighted relation model (cite) and probabilistic coherent spaces (PCOH). The starting point of our approach lies in the apparently basic observation that the morphisms in the relational method can be represented as formal power series (here, cite the work about tropical lambda calculus; maybe also the one pointed out by reviewer B?). In particular, imagine having a program $\gphors$ of finite type $A$, i.e that can reduce only to a finite set of objects $\model{A}=\{a_1, \dots a_k\}$; then we will be able to represent (the intepretation of) $\gphors$ as a family of $n$ power series $A_k(z) = \sum_n \PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)z^n$, $\PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)$ being the probability of $\gphors$ reducing to the value $a_k$ in $n$ steps.\\ 
If we are only interested in termination, considering a unary output ground type $\1$ might suffice: this will lead us to the domain of PHORS (Probabilistic Higher Order Recursion Schemes) (cite), a probabilistic equivalent to the HORS which have been studied with great success in rapport to the problem of higher order model checking. If we are interested in to the posterior distribution of the computed value, thus considering the problem of exact inference, we will on the other side adopt output types that have multiple values (for example, booleans).\\
In any case, there is a striking similarity between the series arising by the (weighted) relational semantics of linear logic and the concept of generating function, that is classical in enumerative combinatorics and probability theory. Our general proposal is then that the techniques developed in the aforementioned fields to deal with generating functions can be used to establish interesting results about probabilistic programs, in particular with respect to the already cited problems of probabilistic model checking and exact inference; in particular, this approach asks us to find class of programs that give rise to 'generating functions' that are treatable by the analytic and algebraic tools developed in combinatorics (cite Flajeolet). It should be noted that this approach has been adopted in the field of imperative programming in (cite Katoen et al), but not in functional programming. An essay of this approach is diplayed in our recent paper, accepted at LICS (cite arxiv), where we are able to extend a result of (Li et al) about probabilistic model checking by means of these techniques. 
\section{Finiteness, algebraicity and rationality}
In combinatorics, interesting results arise when generating functions, which are objects of infinitary nature, can be specified via some finite specification: this is the case, for example, when the can be expressed as the ratio of two polynomials (we speak the of \emph{rational power series}) or when they are solution of (a system of) algebraic equations ( \emph{rational power series}). In programming fixpoints $\gphors \bydef \fix F$ can be seen both as the least solution of the equation $\gphors = F(\gphors)$. When interpreted in the weighted relational model, unluckily this gives rise to an infinite system $\model \gphors = (\model F)(\model \gphors)$ of equation involving analytic function; if appropriate restrictions are placed on $F$, it although possible to prove that the least solution of the systems affects value 0 to all but finitely many of the variables of $\gphors$. In our LICS paper, we introduce such a semantic restriction to this effect: the notion of \emph{finitary family}:
\begin{prop}
	If $\model F(\vec x, \vec y)$ is a finitary family of power series and $\vec t(\vec y) \bydef \fix_x \model {F}$ , then all but finitely many components of $\vec t( \vec y)$ are equal to 0 and the remaining ones are solution of family of \emph{fixpoint algebraic equations} $(\phi_i = p_i(\phi_1, \dots \phi_n, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots n\}}$
\end{prop}
Now, the main point is to specify classes of programs such that the fixpoint operator satisfies the previous algebraic condition. In our LICS paper, we realize that to this aim the logic of graded exponential can be used. We consider both finitary and infinitary exponentials: 
\begin{align*}
	\varphi,\psi&:= o^n\mid !_k\varphi\multimap \varphi\qquad (1\leq n\in \N, k\in \N)\\
	\Phi,\Psi&:=\varphi\mid !_k\varphi\multimap \Phi\qquad (k\in \N\cup\{\infty\})
\end{align*}
the intuitive meaning of $t: !_k\varphi\multimap \psi$ being that $t$ uses its argument at most $k$ times.\\
We then consider judgements: $\nonterm\mid\C P\mid\emptyset\vdash \lambda x_1.\dots. x_n.t:\Phi$ where $\nonterm$ is an intuitionistic context containing the variables $L_1: \Psi_1, \dots L_k:\Psi_k$ over which we want to perform the fixpoint, $\C P$ is a context of parameters (i.e free variables), made of bindings $p:_\infty \phi$ (i.e paramters must have a finitary type, but can be used an infinite numeber of times) and $\Delta$ is a graded context, made of bindings of finitary type $w:_k \phi \: k < +\infty$. At every fixpoint iteration, the partial definition of the $L_i$ gets updated. To find a condition ensuring that $t_F \bydef \lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x. t$ is intepreted by a finitary family of power series, we the introduce a partial order relation over types $\sqsubseteq$ which mirrors the inclusion relation between the interpretation of type in the relational model. We then get that if the type assigned to $\langle L_1 \dots L_n \rangle$ after the iteration $\Phi$ is smaller or equal than the type $\Psi_1 \& \dots \& \Psi_k$ that was assigned to them before the iteration, then  $\model{\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ is a finitary family.

Recent years have seen a surge of interest for probabilistic programming. Studying this kind of program poses difficult challenges already if we only consider discrete probabilistic choices (i.e Bernoulli random variables): as opposed to deterministic programs, each execution of a probabilistic program can give a different result both in term of convergence and of output; as opposed to non-deterministic programs, in probabilistic programs it is essential to keep track of quantitative properties: for example, a probabilstic program can have even infinitely many execution paths which are not terminating and still have probability of termination 1.\\
The need of tracking quantitative information (and, in particular the number of time a probabilistic choice has been duplicated) hints that linear logic, with its resource-wise approach, offers a useful framework in this field. This has been confirmed by the work on the weighted relation model \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LairdMMP13} and probabilistic coherent spaces (PCOH) \cite{DBLP:journals/jacm/EhrhardPT18}. The starting point of our approach lies in the apparently basic observation that the morphisms in the relational method can be represented as formal power series \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2501-15637}, \cite{LAMARCHE199237}. In particular, imagine having a program $\gphors$ of finite type $A$, i.e that can reduce only to a finite set of objects $\model{A}=\{a_1, \dots a_k\}$; then we will be able to represent (the intepretation of) $\gphors$ as a family of $n$ power series $A_k(z) = \sum_n \PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)z^n$, $\PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)$ being the probability of $\gphors$ reducing to the value $a_k$ in $n$ steps.\\ 
If we are only interested in termination, considering a unary output ground type $\1$ might suffice: this will lead us to the domain of PHORS (Probabilistic Higher Order Recursion Schemes) \cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/KobayashiLG20}, a probabilistic equivalent to the HORS which have been studied with regard to the problem of higher order model checking.\\
A seminal result in this area \cite{10.1109/LICS.2006.38} states that it is decidable if the execution of a certain HORS satisfies any MSO expressible property. On the contrary, in the probabilistic case, it has been shown establishing if a give PHORS terminates with probability one is undecidable already at order 2 \cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/KobayashiLG20}. It is then interesting to study for which classes of PHORS this fundamental probabilistic verification problem is decidable, as, for example, the 'affine PHORS' \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LiMO22}\\  
Going beyond termination, the problem of exact inference asks to find the posterior distribution of the values computed by a program,thus involving output types that have multiple values (for example, booleans). Also in this case, even if the considered domain is finite, in the presence of unbounded iteration (and hence the possibility of non-termination), the problem turns out to be undecidable \cite{torresruiz_et_al:LIPIcs.FSCD.2024.20}. \\
The striking similarity between the series arising by the (weighted) relational semantics of linear logic and the concept of generating function, that is classical in enumerative combinatorics and probability theory suggests a framework to find classes of programs for which these generally undecidable problems become computable. Our general proposal is then that the techniques developed in analytic combinatorics \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751} to deal with generating functions can be used to establish interesting results about probabilistic programs, in particular with respect to the already cited problems of probabilistic model checking and exact inference. It should be noted that this approach has been adopted in the field of imperative programming \cite{Klinkenberg2024}, but not in functional programming. An essay of this approach is displayed in our recent paper, accepted at LICS \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted}, where we are able to extend a result of \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LiMO22} about probabilistic model checking by means of these techniques. In section 1 of this abstract, we briefly recap this result; in section 2 and 3 we present some new applications of this approach and in section 4 we outline a possible extension to the problem of estimating the rate of convergence of a program.
\section{Finiteness and algebraicity}
In combinatorics, interesting results arise when generating functions, which are objects of infinitary nature, can be specified via some finite specification: this is the case, for example, when the can be expressed as the ratio of two polynomials (we speak the of \emph{rational power series}) or when they are solution of (a system of) algebraic equations ( \emph{rational power series}). In programming fixpoints $\gphors \bydef \fix F$ can be seen both as the least solution of the equation $\gphors = F(\gphors)$. When interpreted in the weighted relational model, unluckily this gives rise to an infinite system $\model \gphors = (\model F)(\model \gphors)$ of equation involving analytic function; if appropriate restrictions are placed on $F$, it although possible to prove that the least solution of the systems affects value 0 to all but finitely many of the variables of $\gphors$. In \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted}, we introduce a semantic restriction to this effect: the notion of \emph{finitary family}:
\begin{prop}
	If $\model F(\vec x, \vec y)$ is a finitary family of power series and $\vec t(\vec y) \bydef \fix_x \model {F}$ , then all but finitely many components of $\vec t( \vec y)$ are equal to 0 and the remaining ones are solution of family of \emph{fixpoint algebraic equations} $(\phi_i = p_i(\phi_1, \dots \phi_n, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots n\}}$
\end{prop}
Now, the main point is to specify classes of programs such that the fixpoint operator satisfies the previous algebraic condition; we can use to this aim the logic of graded exponential. We will now recap the result of \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted}, which are formulated in the context of PHORS: we consider a unitary ground type $\1$  and we also have just one constant of type $\1$, named $e$. We will show later that these restrictions are not essential and that we can take any finitary ground type $\alpha$, thus examining the problem of exact inference. We introduce a type system $\Ginf$ that involves both finitary and infinitary exponentials: 
\begin{align*}
	\varphi,\psi:= o^n\mid !_k\varphi\multimap \varphi\qquad (1\leq n\in \N, k\in \N) \qquad
	\Phi,\Psi:=\varphi\mid !_k\varphi\multimap \Phi\qquad (k\in \N\cup\{\infty\})
\end{align*}
the intuitive meaning of $t: !_k\varphi\multimap \psi$ being that $t$ uses its argument at most $k$ times.\\
We then consider judgements: $\nonterm\mid\C P\mid\emptyset\vdash \lambda x_1.\dots. x_n.t:\Phi$ where $\nonterm$ is an intuitionistic context containing the variables $L_1: \Psi_1, \dots L_k:\Psi_k$ over which we want to perform the fixpoint, $\C P$ is a context of parameters (i.e free variables), made of bindings $p:_\infty \phi$ (i.e paramters must have a finitary type, but can be used an infinite numeber of times) and $\Delta$ is a graded context, made of bindings of finitary type $w:_k \phi \: k < +\infty$. At every fixpoint iteration, the partial definition of the $L_i$ gets updated. To find a condition ensuring that $t_F \bydef \lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x. t$ is intepreted by a finitary family of power series, we the introduce a partial order relation over types $\sqsubseteq$ which mirrors the inclusion relation between the interpretation of type in the relational model. We then get that if the type assigned to $\langle L_1 \dots L_n \rangle$ after the iteration $\Phi$ is smaller or equal than the type $\Psi_1 \& \dots \& \Psi_k$ that was assigned to them before the iteration, then  $\model{\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ is a finitary family. The system $\Ginf$ allows for taking fixpoints of terms $s: \Phi \multimap \Psi$ exactly when $\Psi \sqsubseteq \Phi$; we thus get:
\begin{theorem}
	If ${\fix_{\vec L }\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ is typable in $\Ginf$, then the finitely many nonzero components of $\model{\fix_{\vec L }\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ are the least solution $(\bar \phi_i( z, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots m\}}$ of an algebraic system $(\phi_i = p_i(\phi_1, \dots \phi_n, z, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots m\}}$.  the variables $\vec y$ correspond to the free paramters $p_1, \dots p_h$ in $\C P$; if we take terms $u_1 \dots u_h$ of adequate type, we get that $\model{\fix_{\vec L }\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t[u_1/p_1 \dots u_h/p_h]}=\phi_i( z, \vec {\model{u_i}}) $
\end{theorem}
While the variables $\vec y$ are used to take into account free parameters that could be substituted afterwards, the (exponent of the) variables $z$ counts the number of probabilistic choices made in the reduction: for example, in the context in which the LICS paper we deal with the case of PHORS: in this case we always assume that $L_1: \1$, so that its interpretation is a single power series (generating function) $g_{L_1}(\vec y, z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)(\vec y)z^n$ (the probability of reducing to $e$ in $n$ steps will obviously depend on the values given to the free variables). We get the following
\begin{theorem}
	For a PHORS typable in $\Ginf$, the generating function $g_{L_1}(\vec y, z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)(\vec y)z^n$ is part of the least the solution of an algebraic system of equations; in particular it is algebraic (i.e there is a polynomial $p(z, w)$ with rational coefficients such that $p(z, g_{L_1})=0$).
\end{theorem}
This result not only is interesting, as it extends to the PHORS typable in $\Ginf$ the Chomsky-Schutzenberger enumeration theorem that is known to hold for context-free languages (corresponding to PHORS of the first order), but, as we will see, paves the way toward asymptotic estimates about the probability of convergence. Moreover, by using Tarski's decidability of the first-order existential theory of the reals, from Theorem 1 we can prove:
\begin{theorem}
	The two following fundamental problems of probabilistic model checking are decidable for $\Gfin$ :
	\begin{varitemize}
		\item Is it true $\PROB(L_1 \to^* e)=1$ (Almost Sure Termination, AST)
		\item Is it true that the expected time to termination $\mathbb{E}{(L_1 \to^* e)} = \sum_{i \geq 0} i \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)$ is finite? (Positive Almost Sure Termination, PAST)
	\end{varitemize}
\end{theorem}
This results extend the ones of \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LiMO22}, who consider an affine type system, that, in respect to ours, only allows for finitary types $!_1 \tau \multimap \tau$. For further detail, see (cite arxiv). In the rest of the abstract, we will show how to extend these results along three direction: finding a type system that ensures rationality of the generating functions, applying this framework to inference and lastly obtaining and interpreting asymptotic estimates on $\PROB(L_1 \to^n e)$.  
\section{Rational PHORS}
If we change the structure of contexts of the $\Ginf$ system, we are able to find a system $\GRat$ ensuring that the generating function $g_{L_1}$ is rational. To this aim, we consider type judgement $\nonterm\mid\C P\mid\emptyset\vdash \lambda x_1.\dots. x_n.t:\Phi$ where $\nonterm$ is now an affine context: no variable is allowed to appear in it with multiplicity greater than one; in particular, the application rule: 
\begin{align*}
\infer[@]{\nonterm \sqcup \nonterm' \mid\C P\mid \Delta + k\Delta' \vdash tu : \Psi}
{\nonterm\mid\C P\mid\Delta \vdash t :!_k\varphi \multimap \Psi \qquad \nonterm'\mid\C P\mid \Delta' \vdash u :\varphi }
\end{align*} cannot be used unless $dom(\nonterm) \cap dom(\nonterm')= \emptyset$. This ensures that the algebraic system describing the fixpoint $(\phi_i = p_i(\phi_1, \dots \phi_n, z, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots m\}}$ is a linear system. Hence, not only the (least) solution $(\bar \phi_i( z, \vec y))_{i \in \{1 \dots m\}}$ consists of rational functions of $ z, \vec y$ , but it is also possible to explicitly compute these solutions by means of the Gauss algorithm:
\begin{theorem}
	If a PHORS is typable in $\GRat$, then: 
	\begin{varitemize}
		\item  The generating function $g_{L_1}(\vec y, z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)(\vec y)z^n$ is rational
		\item If $L_1$ is closed,  the probability of termination $\PROB(L_1 \to^* e)$ is a rational number and it can be computed effectively from the $\GRat$ typing derivation of the PHORS. Moreover, for PHORS of fixed order, the computation can be performed in polynomial time with respect to the size of the PHORS.
	\end{varitemize}
\end{theorem}  
\section{Algebraic functions for inference}
The results of the two previous paragraph can be easily extended to the case in which we have a finitary ground type $\alpha$ and constant $a_1 : \alpha \dots a_n: \alpha$; for simplicity, we can consider the booleans $\Bool$ and constants $\False: \Bool, \True: \Bool$.
A closed term $t:\Bool$ in this language will reduce with probability $p$ to $\False$ and with probability $q$ to $\True$ (with $p+q \leq 1$); we will then say that $t \sim \Bern{p}{q}$. The problem of exact inference is then to find these $p,q$. 
 The language will then contain also an $\IfThenELse$ construct. The system $\Ginf$ can then easily be replaced by a system $\Flipinf$ in which the fixpoint rule is subjected to the same type containment constraint as $\Ginf$; by taking judgements $\nonterm\mid\C P\mid\emptyset\vdash \lambda x_1.\dots. x_n.t:\Phi$ where $\nonterm$ is affine context we can build a system $\FlipRat$ ensuring rationality . We will then get: \begin{theorem}
 	If a term is typable in $\Flipinf$, then: 
 	\begin{varitemize}
 		\item  For $b \in \{\False, \True\}$, the generating function $g_{L_1,b}(\vec y, z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i b)(\vec y)z^n$ is algebraic.
 		\item If $L_1$ is closed and $L_1 \sim \Bern{p}{q}$, then, for any given $0 < \epsilon \in \Q$ we can compute effectively approximations $p', q'$ such that $|p-p'| < \epsilon, |q-q'| < \epsilon $ 	
 	\end{varitemize}
 	 If a term is typable in $\FlipRat$:
 	  	\begin{varitemize}
 	 	\item  For $b \in \{\False, \True\}$, the generating function $g_{L_1,b}(\vec y, z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i b)(\vec y)z^n$ is rational.
 	 	\item If $L_1$ is closed and $L_1 \sim \Bern{p}{q}$, then $p$ and $q$ are rational and can be computed effectively.
 	 \end{varitemize}
 \end{theorem}
 \section{Asymptotic estimates}
 The fact that the generating function of a PHORS $g_{L_1}(z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)z^n$ is algebraic let us not just to answer the AST problem, but also establishing how fast the program converges. This analysis, that is an application of the methods of analytic combinatorics \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751},  is yet to be developed, but we will sketch here the basic idea. If the radius of convergence of the power series $g_{L_1}(z)$ is $\rho > 1$, then $\PROB(L_1 \to^i e)= O(1/\rho^n)$. On the other side, if $\rho = 1$ ($\rho <1$ is impossible to the convergence results for PCOH), the asymptotic behaviours is regulated by the nature of the dominant (i.e smallest in modulus) singularities of $g_{L_1}(z)$: for example, if $g_{L_1}(z)$ has a unique dominant singularity of 'square root type' (i.e it has a Puiseaux expansion of the form $g_{L_1}(z) = \sum_{k \geq 1} c_k (z-1)^{k/2}$), one obtains that $\PROB(L_1 \to^i e)= O(n^{3/2})$\\
 While the problem of finding the radius of convergence and the Puiseaux expansions is generally uncomputable, when the function is algebraic (so there exists an irreducible polynomial $p(z,w)$ s.t $p(z,g_{L_1}(z)$) singularities are located at the points $(z,w)$ for which $p(z,w)= \frac{\partial p}{\partial w} = 0$ and there are algorithm to compute the radius of convergence and the Puiseaux expansions \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751}, \cite{CHUDNOVSKY1986271}. We obtain for example that:
 \begin{prop}
  Let ${\fix_{\vec L }\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ be typable in $\Ginf$ and let $p(z,w)$ be the irreducible annihilating polynomial of $g_{L_1}(z)$ (that can be computed effectively). Let $\PROB(L_1 \to^{\leq n} e)$ be the probability of termination \emph{after $n$ steps}. If for no $|z|<1$ we can find a $w$ s.t $p(z,w)= \frac{\partial p}{\partial w} = 0$, then there exists a $r < 1$ s.t  $\PROB(L_1 \to^{*} e) - \PROB(L_1 \to^{\leq n} e) = O(r^n)$ for $n \to + \infty$.  
 \end{prop}

%More recently (cite Kobayashi-Dal Lago), these Higher Order Recursion Schemes have been extended to the probabilistic setting by the formalism of Probabilistic Higher Order Recursion Schemes (PHORS): the most basic problems of model-checking become that of establishing if a program terminates with probability 1 (Almost Sure Termination, AST) and if the expected number of steps before termination is finite (Positive Almost Sure Termination, PAST). AST has been proved to be undecidable already at order 2, which prompted the study of classes of HORS for which the problem of (P)AST is decidable. A notable result in this direction has been obtained by (Li et al), which prove by automata-theoretic techniques that (P)AST is decidable for PHORS subject to an "affine" typing restriction (i.e programs are allowed to use their input only once).\\

In particular, we will show that to each PHORS we can associate a generating function, encoding its termination behaviour and then we will define type discipline that ensure this generating function to be \textit{rational} or \textit{algebraic}. In both cases we will be able to decide the (P)AST problem; in the former case, we will also be able to compute the exact probability of termination, while in the latter we will still get asymptotic estimates on the rate of convergence of the program.

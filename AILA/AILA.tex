\section{Introduction}
The recent years have seen a surge of interest towards probabilistic programming. Probabilistic programs pose difficult challenges already when one only considers discrete probabilistic choices (i.e.~Bernoulli random variables): as opposed to deterministic programs, each execution of a probabilistic program can give a different result both in term of convergence and of its output; hence, while for non-deterministic programs one can study \emph{qualitative} properties (does the program terminate or not?), for probabilistic programs one has to consider \emph{quantitative} versions of such properties (what is the probability that the program terminates?). 
%Studying such properties  essential to keep track of quantitative properties: for example, a probabilstic program can have even infinitely many execution paths which are not terminating and still have probability of termination 1.

However, quantitative properties can be difficult or even intractable in many contexts.
For instance, the literature on \emph{probabilistic model-checking} has studied the problems to determine the probability of termination or the expected running time for different computational models, leading to several results, both positive and negative, regarding the decidability and tractability of the underlying verification problem.
Beyond termination, one can consider the problem of \emph{exact inference}, that is, of finding the posterior distribution of the values computed by a program. While efficient methods are known for finitary programs, in the presence of \emph{unbounded} iteration (and hence the possibility of non-termination), inference is undecidable \cite{torresruiz_et_al:LIPIcs.FSCD.2024.20}, but
a clear picture of the decidable or tractable cases is still missing.

Properties like the probability of termination or the expected running time are often sensitive to how much the program is able to \emph{duplicate} its inputs. This is why ideas and methods from linear logic have been widely applied in this area. 
%
%The need of tracking quantitative information (and, in particular the number of time a probabilistic choice has been duplicated) suggests that linear logic, with its resource-wise approach, offers a useful framework in this field. 
In particular, the recent literature has explored methods based on the weighted relational model \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LairdMMP13} and probabilistic coherent spaces (PCOH) \cite{DBLP:journals/jacm/EhrhardPT18}. 
The starting point of our approach lies in the basic observation that the interpretation of programs in both such models can be represented as formal power series \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2501-15637}, \cite{LAMARCHE199237}. For example, consider a program $\gphors$ that can reduce to a finite set of outputs $\model{A}=\{a_1, \dots a_k\}$; then the interpretation of $\gphors$ yields a family of $n$ \emph{generating functions} $A_k(z) = \sum_n \PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)z^n$, where $\PROB(\gphors \to^n a_k)$ indicates the probability of $\gphors$ reducing to the value $a_k$ in $n$ steps.

Our goal is to explore the use of the concept of generating function, that is classical in enumerative and analytic combinatorics  \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751}, to identify classes of probabilistic programs whose quantitative properties can be extracted, in a computable way, from their interpretation in the weighted relational model or PCOH. 
%
%Our general proposal is then that the techniques developed in analytic combinatorics \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751} to deal with generating functions can be used to establish interesting results about probabilistic programs, in particular with respect to the already cited problems of probabilistic model checking and exact inference.
While the combinatorial approach has already been explored in the field of imperative programming \cite{Klinkenberg2024}, these linear logic models provide convenient tools to extend it to \emph{higher-order} programming languages.

 In the recent paper \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted} we provided a first demonstration of this method by reproving (and extending) a result of \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LiMO22} about probabilistic model checking using algebraic power series. In this abstract we first briefly recall this result, and then sketch a few new results and perspectives 
arising from this method.

\section{PHORS, aka. the Probabilistic $\lambda Y$-Calculus}


The literature on higher-order model checking has focused on the language of HORS (Higher Order Recursion Schemes), due to a seminal result \cite{10.1109/LICS.2006.38} stating that all properties of HORS expressible in monadic second order logic (including termination) are decidable.  
HORS are programs defined as fixpoints of a finite system of recursive equations of the form $Fx_1\dots x_n=t$. 
The functional variables $F$ defined by such equations are called the \emph{non-terminal symbols} of the PHORS. 
In the probabilistic HORS, or PHORS, introduced in \cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/KobayashiLG20}, equations are of the form $Fx_1\dots x_n=t_L\oplus_p t_R$, involving a probabilistic choice operator $\oplus_p$.
The execution of a (P)HORS starts from a \emph{source} non-terminal symbol $S$ and goes on by reading the equations  from left to right as reduction rules. 
For example, the PHORS in Fig.~\ref{fig:phors1} simulates a simple random walk terminating on a constant $e$.
The (P)HORS are equivalent to the simply typed $\lambda Y$-calculus, that is, the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus enriched with a fixpoint operation $Y:(\sigma\to\sigma)\to\sigma$ at all types $\sigma$, enriched with probabilistic choice operators $\oplus_p$ in the probabilistic case.
%
%A probabilistic variant of the HORS, called PHORS, was introduced in \cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/KobayashiLG20}: here a program is defined as the fixpoint of equations of the form $Fx_1\dots x_n=t_L\oplus_p t_R$ involving a probabilistic choice operator $\oplus_p$. This language is then equivalent to an extension of the simply typed $\lambda Y$-calculus with probabilistic operators.
%

While qualitative properties like termination are decidable for the HORS, the corresponding quantitative properties of PHORS are not: establishing if a PHORS terminates with probability 1 (\emph{almost sure termination}, AST) is undecidable already at order 2 \cite{DBLP:journals/lmcs/KobayashiLG20}. It is then interesting to study for which classes of PHORS problems like AST (or the related PAST problem, asking whether the expected running time is finite) are tractable or, at least, decidable.

To study exact inference we also consider an extension of the PHORS language comprising a type $\Bool$ for Booleans. This allows one to encode not only finite graphical models like e.g.~Bayesian networks, but also \emph{unbounded} models \cite[Ch.~6]{Daphne2009}, based on the iteration of some basic network template, as in the example in Fig.~\ref{fig:phors2}.
A Boolean PHORS $\gphors$ reduces with probability $p$ to $\False$ and with probability $q$ to $\True$ (with $p+q \leq 1$). We note this $\gphors\sim \Bern{p}{q}$. The (generally undecidable) problem of exact inference is then to find these $p,q$.

\section{Finiteness and Algebraicity}



%A PHORS is a program defined as a fixpoint of a system of program equations. 
%
%In programming fixpoints $\gphors \bydef \fix F$ can be seen both as the least solution of the equation $\gphors = F(\gphors)$.
The weighted relational model of linear logic is a well-studied denotational semantics in which a program $M:\sigma\to\tau$ is interpreted as a matrix $\model{M}:!\model{\sigma}\times\model{\tau}\to \Qsemiring$ with coefficients in a continuous semiring $\Qsemiring$, where $\model{\sigma},\model{\tau}$ are countable sets and $!X$ denotes the set of finite multiset on $X$. This matrix can be equivalently seen as a family of formal power series $\model{M}( x)_b= \sum_{\mu\in !\model{\sigma}}\model{M}_{\mu,b}x^{\mu}$, where for all $x\in \Qsemiring^{\model{\sigma}}$, $x^\mu=\prod_{a\in \model{\sigma}}x_a^{\mu(a)}$.


Interpreting a PHORS in this model corresponds then to finding the \emph{least fixpoint} of an \emph{infinite} system $\model \gphors = \Phi(\model \gphors)$ of equations between power series.
In particular, by choosing $\Qsemiring=\fps{\Rinf}{z}$ the semiring of formal power series in $z$ with positive real coefficients, 
the interpretation of the source symbol $S$ of a PHORS yields a generating function
$\model{S}(z)= \sum_{n \geq 0} \PROB(\gphors \Downarrow_n)z^n$ characterizing the 
probability of termination in $n$ reduction steps.

Unfortunately, the infinitary systems produced by the PHORS are not, in general, tractable with algebraic or computational tools. However, what if we could ensure, by some appropriate restrictions on the PHORS $\gphors$, that the resulting equational system is equivalent to some \emph{finite} polynomial system? This is the approach we follow  
%  it although possible to prove that the least solution of the systems affects value 0 to all but finitely many of the variables of $\gphors$. In
in  \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted}, where we introduce a semantic condition of \emph{finiteness} to this effect. For the technical notion of \emph{finitary family} see \cite{lago2026higherorderprobabilisticverificationweighted}; we here report its main property:
% the notion of \emph{finitary family}:
\begin{prop}
	Suppose $(\Phi_i(\vec x, \vec y))_{i\in I}$ is a finitary family of power series. If $r_i(\vec y)$ is the least solution of 
	the system $r_i(\vec x)=\Phi_i(r_i(\vec x),\vec y)$, then all but finitely many of the $r_i( \vec y)$ are equal to 0 and the remaining ones are solution of a finite family of {polynomial equations} $(r_{j_\ell}(\vec x) = p_\ell(r_{j_1}(\vec x), \dots r_{j_n}(\vec x), \vec y))_{\ell \in \{1 \dots n\}}$.
\end{prop}
Now, the main point is to identify a class of PHORS whose interpretation satisfies the previous condition.
We do this by introducing the class of \emph{graded PHORS} (PHORS$^\infty$), which extends the \emph{affine PHORS}from \cite{DBLP:conf/lics/LiMO22}. The graded PHORS are defined using a linear type system involving \emph{graded exponentials} (where a graded type of the form $!_k\varphi\multimap \psi$ describes a program that can use an input of type $\varphi$ \emph{at most $k$ times} to produce an output of type $\psi$).
More precisely, a type judgement in this system is of the form $\nonterm\mid \Delta\vdash t:\sigma$, where:
\begin{varitemize}
\item $\nonterm$ is an \emph{intuitionistic} (i.e.~non-linear) environment for the non-terminal symbols;
\item $\Delta$ is a \emph{graded} environment all the other variables, comprising \emph{both} finite and infinite grades.
\end{varitemize}
The type system is designed so as to do two things: first, restrict the use of variables with an infinite grade (which can thus be duplicated at will), as these may only play the role of the \emph{parameters} $\vec y$ in the power series $r_i(\vec y)$ above; 
second, enforce a fixpoint condition ensuring that for any equation $Fx_1\dots x_n=t$, 
the non-terminal function symbol $F$ and the term $\lambda x_1\dots x_n.t$ agree on the grades assigned to $x_1,\dots, x_n$; this condition warrants that the least fixpoint $r_i(\vec y)$ is determined by only a \emph{finite} number of equations. This leads to the following:

\paragraph{Rational PHORS}

It is well-known that algebraic numbers are computable, in the sense of being approximable to arbitrary degree. 
Similarly, algebraic power series come with several well-known exact or approximate computational methods. 
However, just like rational numbers are computable in an exact way, stronger (i.e.~more tractable) methods exist when considering rational power series, that is power series that can be expressed as a ratio of two polynomials.

Starting from the type system described above, it is not difficult to design a more restricted discipline which enforces rationality. Indeed, it is well-known that rational power series $r(\vec y)=\frac{p(\vec y)}{q(\vec y)}$ can be characterized as the solution of \emph{linear} recursive systems $X_i= a_i(\vec y)X_i+ b_i(\vec y)$ \cite{DBLP:books/sp/KuichS86}. 
Such systems can be solved effectively (and efficiently) by means of the Gauss algorithm \cite{LEHMANN197759}. 

In our system, this linearity constraint can be ensured by restricting the discipline of non-terminal symbols: 
in the typing context $\nonterm\mid\Delta\vdash t\sigma$ we now require that the variables in $\nonterm$ be used in an \emph{affine} way, that is, possibly discarded but never duplicated. 
Call $\GRat$ the class of PHORS typable in this restricted system. We then have the following

 \paragraph{Asymptotic Estimates}
 The algebraicity of the generating function of a PHORS 
% $g_{L_1}(z)= \sum_{i \geq 0} \PROB(L_1 \to^i e)z^n$
  is not only useful to answer exact problems like AST, but also to estimate \emph{how fast} the program converges. We provide here a sketch of how, using the methods from analytic combinatorics \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751}, it might be possible to make such estimates. If the radius of convergence of the power series $\model{\gphors}(z)$ is $\rho > 1$, then $\PROB(\gphors\Downarrow_n)= O(1/\rho^n)$. On the other side, if $\rho = 1$,
  (the case $\rho <1$ can be excluded by standard convergence results), 
   the asymptotic behaviors is regulated by the nature of the dominant (i.e.~smallest in modulus) singularities of $\model{\gphors}(z)$: for example, if $\model{S}(z)$ has a unique dominant singularity of 'square root type' (i.e.~it has a Puiseaux expansion of the form $\model{S}(z)= \sum_{k \geq 1} c_k (z-1)^{k/2}$), one obtains that $\PROB({\gphors}\Downarrow_n)= O(n^{3/2})$\\
 While the problem of finding the radius of convergence and the Puiseaux expansions is generally not computable, when the function is algebraic (so there exists an irreducible polynomial $p(z,w)$ s.t $p(z,\model{S}(z))=0$) singularities are located at the points $(z,w)$ for which $\frac{\partial p(z,w)}{\partial w} = 0$ and algorithms exist to compute both \cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023751}, \cite{CHUDNOVSKY1986271}. We obtain for example that:
 \begin{prop}
 Let $\gphors$ be a $\Ginf$ and 
%  Let ${\fix_{\vec L }\lambda \vec L \lambda \vec x .t}$ be typable in $\Ginf$ and
   let $p(z,w)$ be the irreducible annihilating polynomial of $\model{\gphors}(z)$ (that can be computed effectively). Let $\PROB(\gphors\Downarrow_{< n} )$ be the probability of termination \emph{in less than $n$ steps}. If for no $|z|<1$ we can find a $w$ s.t $ \frac{\partial p(z,w)}{\partial w} = 0$, then there exists a $r < 1$ s.t  $\PROB(\gphors\Downarrow_n) = \PROB(\gphors\Downarrow_{< n}) + O(r^n)$ for $n \to + \infty$.  
 \end{prop}
